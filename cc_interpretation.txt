Interpretation of Results

Sample: N = 116 after exclusions (31 unprompted, 38 sycophantic, 47 contrarian). Mean age 42.8 (SD = 12.9), roughly balanced gender (52.6% male, 47.4% female). All scale reliabilities are excellent (all alphas > .91).

---

H1: Opinion Change — NOT SUPPORTED

No significant differences across conditions. ANOVA: F(2,113) = 1.04, p = .358, eta-squared = .018. Kruskal-Wallis: chi-squared(2) = 1.11, p = .573. No pairwise comparisons are significant.

Interestingly, only the unprompted condition showed a significant within-condition shift (M_diff = 0.55, p = .024). The sycophantic (0.08, p = .668) and contrarian (0.19, p = .427) conditions did not. This is the opposite of the predicted ordering.

H2: Confidence Change — NOT SUPPORTED

ANOVA: F(2,113) = 0.78, p = .462, eta-squared = .014. No significant differences. The sycophantic condition did show a significant within-condition confidence increase (M = +0.47, p = .009), but the between-group comparison is non-significant.

H3: Trust — SUPPORTED

Significant effect: F(2,113) = 6.63, p = .002, eta-squared = .105 (medium effect). The contrarian condition had significantly lower trust than both unprompted (Tukey p = .009) and sycophantic (Tukey p = .006). Unprompted and sycophantic did not differ. Confirmed by Kruskal-Wallis (p = .003) and Dunn tests.

H4: Satisfaction — SUPPORTED

F(2,113) = 7.19, p = .001, eta-squared = .113 (medium). The contrarian condition had lower satisfaction than unprompted (Tukey p = .030) and especially sycophantic (Tukey p = .001). Kruskal-Wallis confirms (p = .002). Dunn test shows the sycophantic-contrarian contrast is the strongest (p = .002).

H5: Credibility — SUPPORTED

F(2,113) = 8.00, p < .001, eta-squared = .124 (medium-large). Contrarian was rated significantly less credible than unprompted (Tukey p = .001) and sycophantic (p = .008). Kruskal-Wallis: p < .001. Again, the main driver is the contrarian condition being rated lower.

H6: Competence — NOT SUPPORTED

F(2,113) = 2.01, p = .139, eta-squared = .034. The trend is in the expected direction (contrarian lowest), but it does not reach significance.

H7: Continuance — SUPPORTED (marginally)

F(2,113) = 4.04, p = .020, eta-squared = .067 (small-medium). Contrarian significantly lower than unprompted (Tukey p = .041). Contrarian vs. sycophantic is marginal (Tukey p = .056). Kruskal-Wallis: p = .033. However, Dunn post-hocs with Bonferroni correction lose significance (p = .072/.098), so this is a borderline finding.

H8: Interaction Time — NOT SUPPORTED

F(2,113) = 0.32, p = .724, eta-squared = .006. No differences whatsoever. Conditions spent essentially the same amount of time.

H9: Manipulation Check (Perceived Sycophancy) — STRONGLY SUPPORTED

F(2,113) = 54.96, p < 2e-16, eta-squared = .493 (very large effect). All three conditions differ significantly from each other (all Tukey p < .001). Means: sycophantic = 4.07, unprompted = 3.03, contrarian = 1.78. This validates that participants clearly perceived the experimental manipulation — the sycophantic model was seen as most agreeable, and the contrarian as least.

---

Summary Pattern

The manipulation clearly worked (H9). However, the conditions did not differentially shift opinions (H1) or confidence (H2). Where the conditions did differ was in user experience: participants in the contrarian condition rated the AI as less trustworthy (H3), less satisfying (H4), less credible (H5), and were less willing to continue using it (H7). The sycophantic and unprompted conditions were generally rated similarly on these UX measures. Competence (H6) showed the same trend but didn't reach significance.

In short: being contrarian hurts the user experience without actually changing people's minds, while being sycophantic doesn't meaningfully reinforce beliefs more than a neutral baseline — but it also doesn't damage the user's perception of the AI.
